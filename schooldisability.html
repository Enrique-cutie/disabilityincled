Step-by-Step Teaching Guides

Select a disability type below to read the step-by-step teaching guide and real classroom scenario for that learner.

This system includes structured, step-by-step teaching guides that provide clear and practical instructions for teachers in handling inclusive classrooms. These guides focus on what teachers should do before, during, and after class when teaching students with different types of disabilities.

For example, in teaching students with visual impairment (blind learners), teachers are guided as follows:

Step 1: Classroom Preparation — Ensure the classroom is safe and free from obstacles. Assign appropriate seating near the teacher. Prepare audio-based or accessible learning materials.

Step 2: Lesson Delivery — Read aloud all written content on the board. Clearly describe visual materials such as images, charts, or diagrams. Use verbal explanations consistently throughout the lesson.

Step 3: Student Interaction — Address the student by name before speaking. Provide clear and direct verbal instructions. Check for understanding through oral responses.

Step 4: Learning Activities — Provide alternative activities such as audio tasks or guided assistance. Allow the use of assistive tools when necessary.

Step 5: Assessment — Conduct oral examinations instead of written tests. Accept audio-recorded responses as outputs.

Real Classroom Scenario

Scenario: A classroom of 50 students, including 2 students with hearing impairment and 1 student with visual impairment.

Guided Teaching Approach: The teacher begins the lesson by combining verbal explanation and visual presentation. All written content on the board is read aloud to support the blind student. Key points are written clearly to assist deaf students. Peer support is encouraged by assigning classmates to assist students with special needs.

Assessment Approach: Deaf students are given written or visual-based tasks. Blind students are assessed through oral or audio responses.

For students with hearing impairment (deaf or hard-of-hearing learners), teachers are guided to prepare visual materials, face the student when speaking, write key points on the board, use basic FSL gestures, and assess through written or visual exams.

Step 1: Classroom Preparation — Prepare visual materials such as slides, printed notes, and diagrams. Assign front seating where the student can see the teacher's face and board clearly.

Step 2: Lesson Delivery — Always face the student when speaking. Write key points clearly on the board. Use simple gestures or basic FSL.

Step 3: Student Interaction — Use visual cues and written instructions. Maintain eye contact. Never speak while facing away.

Step 4: Learning Activities — Provide visual-based activities: reading tasks, diagrams, illustrated worksheets.

Step 5: Assessment — Use written, drawing-based, or multiple-choice examinations.

Real Classroom Scenario

A classroom of 50 students includes 2 students with hearing impairment and 1 with visual impairment. The teacher combines verbal and written presentation simultaneously, pairs deaf students with hearing peers for group activities, and faces the class when speaking at all times.

For students with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD), teachers are guided to create a predictable environment, post visual schedules, give short clear instructions, and warn before transitions.

Step 1: Set up a quiet, organized seating area. Post a clear visual daily schedule on the board.

Step 2: Use short, simple, direct instructions. Give advance notice before any transition. Avoid abstract language and sarcasm.

Step 3: Use the student's name calmly. Speak in a calm, predictable tone.

Step 4: Provide structured, predictable tasks incorporating areas of interest when possible.

Step 5: Use written or structured formats. Provide extended time and a quiet space.

Real Classroom Scenario

A classroom of 50 includes 1 ASD student sensitive to noise. The teacher posts the daily schedule visually, delivers lessons calmly, seats the student away from high-traffic areas, and assesses through written tasks in a quiet environment.

For students with physical or orthopedic disability, teachers ensure accessible pathways, provide adaptive tools, allow extra time for writing, and offer oral or typed assessment alternatives.

Step 1: Ensure clear, accessible pathways and seating. Prepare adaptive tools (pencil grips, book stands).

Step 2: Provide materials in accessible formats. Allow extra time for note-taking.

Step 3: Never assume limitations — always ask. Position at eye level when speaking to wheelchair users.

Step 4: Offer alternative participation: verbal responses, voice recording, or typed answers.

Step 5: Allow oral, typed, or recorded submissions. Maintain high academic expectations.

Real Classroom Scenario

A classroom of 50 includes 1 wheelchair user with limited right-hand use. Content is projected so the student never needs to move to the board. A peer partner assists during group activities. Assessments are typed or oral.

For students with psychosocial or mental health disability, teachers maintain a calm, predictable environment, address concerns privately, offer flexible participation, and refer to the guidance counselor when needed.

Step 1: Set up a calm, predictable, welcoming classroom. Know the student's triggers before the school year.

Step 2: Use a calm, consistent tone. Avoid singling out the student publicly.

Step 3: Address concerns privately. Use supportive, non-judgmental language.

Step 4: Offer flexible participation. Allow brief breaks when the student is overwhelmed.

Step 5: Refer to the guidance counselor when signs of distress persist. Document observations.

Real Classroom Scenario

A classroom of 50 includes 1 student with anxiety who withdraws during oral recitation. The teacher offers written alternatives, checks in privately, maintains a calm corner, and uses portfolio-based assessment.

For students with chronic illness, teachers read the Health Action Plan, allow flexible seating and medication access, provide make-up work, and allow extended deadlines after illness.

Step 1: Read the student's Health Action Plan. Know emergency contacts and medication schedule.

Step 2: Allow flexible seating near the door. Permit water and medication at the desk.

Step 3: Treat the student as a capable learner. Check in privately, not in front of class.

Step 4: Provide make-up work after absences. Allow rest breaks during class.

Step 5: Allow extended deadlines after illness. Offer oral or take-home alternatives.

Real Classroom Scenario

A classroom of 50 includes 1 student with asthma. The teacher seats them near ventilation, prepares lesson summaries for absences, reschedules missed quizzes without penalty, and protects the student's privacy.

For students with intellectual disability, teachers prepare simplified, concrete materials, break lessons into small steps, give one instruction at a time, and assess through oral, practical, or picture-based tasks.

Step 1: Prepare simplified, concrete, visually supported materials. Break lesson into small clear steps.

Step 2: Use simple words and short sentences. Demonstrate with real objects when possible.

Step 3: Use the student's name frequently. Give one instruction at a time and wait for response.

Step 4: Use hands-on, practical, repetitive tasks. Celebrate every effort and achievement.

Step 5: Assess through oral responses, practical demonstrations, or picture-based tasks.

Real Classroom Scenario

A classroom of 50 includes 1 student with mild intellectual disability. The teacher uses concrete objects, breaks instructions into steps, seats the student near the teacher, assigns a peer buddy, and assesses through practical demonstrations.

Blind / Visually Impaired Student

5 steps · Before Class → Assessment

Step 1

Before Class

  • Make sure the classroom is safe and clear
  • Let the student sit near the teacher
Step 2

During Teaching

  • Read everything written on the board
  • Explain pictures and diagrams using words
Step 3

Interaction

  • Call the student by name
  • Give clear verbal instructions
Step 4

Activity

  • Give audio or guided tasks
  • Use tactile or hands-on materials
Step 5

Assessment

  • Use oral exams or voice recordings
  • Allow extended time if needed
These steps help the teacher know exactly what to do before, during, and after class for a blind student.

Student with Physical / Orthopedic Disability

5 steps · Before Class → Assessment

Step 1

Before Class

  • Ensure accessible pathways and seating
  • Prepare adaptive tools (pencil grips, etc.)
Step 2

During Teaching

  • Provide materials in reachable formats
  • Allow extra time for writing tasks
Step 3

Interaction

  • Ask — never assume what they can't do
  • Position yourself at eye level when talking
Step 4

Activity

  • Offer alternative ways to participate
  • Use voice, dictation, or digital tools
Step 5

Assessment

  • Allow oral or typed submissions
  • Adjust time limits as needed
Physical disability does not equal intellectual disability — keep academic expectations high and adapt the format, not the content.

Real Classroom Scenario

This system also includes real classroom examples — so teachers can see inclusive teaching in action.

Example Class:

50 Students total
2 Deaf students
1 Blind student

What the teacher will do:

Explain lesson using both speaking and visuals

So all 50 students — hearing, deaf, and blind — receive the lesson equally.

Read aloud what is written (for blind student)

Verbalize all board content so the blind student gets the same information.

Write key points clearly (for deaf students)

Keep text on the board so deaf students can always follow visually.

Use classmates to help (peer support)

Pair classmates with students who need assistance during activities.

These steps help teachers know exactly what to do in class — giving them clear steps and real examples so they can apply inclusive teaching, not just understand it.

About IncluEd

A free online platform providing structured teacher training for inclusive education nationwide.

Our Mission

Bridging the Training Gap

Inclusive education is mandated nationwide. Yet many teachers lack training to effectively support learners with disabilities. IncluEd bridges this gap with free, structured, and impactful training.

Main Objective

To develop and deliver a comprehensive online teacher training system that prepares educators for inclusive classrooms through structured awareness, guided modules, interactive activities, and validated certification.

Scope

Covers Braille, Filipino Sign Language, Autism, Physical Disability, Psychosocial, Chronic Illness, and Intellectual Disability training tracks with modules, videos, quizzes, and certification.

Limitations

This system focuses on online-only training. It does not replace face-to-face workshops, medical diagnosis, or therapeutic interventions.
